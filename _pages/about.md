I’m a **Ph.D. student** in Ecology and Evolutionary Biology at the **University of Kansas**,  working in the **Colella Lab of Evolutionary & Ecological Genomics** and the **KU Natural History Museum & Biodiversity Institute**. 

I am also lucky enough to say that I am a **Madison and Lila Self Graduate Fellow**. This program is a fully-funded 4-year PhD fellowship with additional professional development opportunities.

My research explores how **mammals and their parasites respond to environmental and anthropogenic change**, integrating parasitology, evolutionary genomics, and museum specimen-based science.  

I’m especially drawn to questions that link the present with the past, specifically using **natural history collections** as time machines to understand how life adapts across space and time.

---

### My journey in biology  

I earned my B.S. in Biology from the **University of New Mexico** in 2021, with minors in **Chemistry and Philosophy**. Interestingly, I think it was my philosophy courses in environmental and land ethics that ultimately shaped my path toward conservation science. It was during these courses that I was introduced to the writings of Aldo Leopold, whose words resonated with me then and continue to influence how I think about the natural world today.

One quote in particular has become central to both my personal values and my approach to scientific research:

> "We abuse land because we see it as a commodity belonging to us. When we see land as a community to which we belong, we may begin to use it with love and respect"  
> — *Aldo Leopold*

After graduation, I worked as a post-baccalaureate researcher in the **Museum of Southwestern Biology’s Division of Mammals**, developing skills in specimen-based research, field collection, and molecular lab techniques.  

Inspired by that work, I went on to complete an **M.S. in Biology** at UNM in 2024, focusing on the **evolutionary history and phylogeography of Eurasian shrews** using genomic datasets and museum specimens with **Dr. Joseph Cook**. In the field, I was always stuck doing guts in the "assembly line" (iykyk) and was always really good at finding endoparasites, but never knew what I was looking at.

Now at **KU,** I continue to merge fieldwork, genomics, and museum data to uncover how species — and their endoparasites — respond to their changing surroundings. I suppose you can call me somewhat of a parasitologist now!

---

### What drives my research  

I have always been fascinated by the Earth's immense biodiversity and the ecological and evolutionary processes that shape it (ex dinosaur kid here). My research is driven by a desire to better understand how species interact with one another, respond to environmental change, and adapt across space and time.

Through my work, I aim to answer questions such as:
- How do parasite communities reflect host health, diet, and ecological interactions?
- How might environmental change influence parasite transmission and zoonotic risk?
- What genomic signatures reveal adaptation to seasonal and environmental variation?

Ultimately, I hope my research contributes to a broader understanding of biodiversity while providing insights that inform **wildlife management and conservation**.

Central to all of this work are **natural history collections**. Museum specimens provide invaluable records of biodiversity across space and time, allowing us to investigate ecological and evolutionary questions that would otherwise be impossible to address.
