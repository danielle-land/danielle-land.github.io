I’m a **Ph.D. student** in Ecology and Evolutionary Biology at the **University of Kansas**,  working in the **Colella Lab of Evolutionary & Ecological Genomics** and the **KU Natural History Museum & Biodiversity Institute**. 

I am also lucky enough to say that I am a **Madison and Lila Self Graduate Fellow** — a fully-funded 4-year PhD fellowship with additional professional development oportunities.

My research explores how **mammals and their parasites respond to environmental and anthropogenic change**, integrating parasitology, evolutionary genomics, and museum-based science.  

I’m especially drawn to questions that link the ecological present with the evolutionary past — using **natural history collections** as time machines to understand how life adapts across space and time.

---

### My journey in biology  

I earned my B.S. in Biology from the **University of New Mexico** in 2021, where I also minored in **Chemistry and Philosophy**. After graduation, I worked as a post-baccalaureate researcher in the **Museum of Southwestern Biology’s Division of Mammals**, developing skills in specimen-based research, field collection, and molecular lab techniques.  

Inspired by that work, I went on to complete an **M.S. in Biology** at UNM in 2024, focusing on the **evolutionary history and phylogeography of Eurasian shrews** using genomic datasets and museum specimens.  

Now at KU, I continue to merge fieldwork, genomics, and museum data to uncover how species — and their parasites — evolve in response to environmental pressures.

---

### What drives my research  

I’m fascinated by how biodiversity, health, and adaptation intertwine.  

Through my work, I aim to answer questions like:  
- How do parasite communities reflect host health and diet?  
- How does zoonotic risk shift across changing landscapes?  
- What genomic signatures reveal adaptation to seasonal or environmental extremes?  

Ultimately, I hope to contribute insights that support **wildlife management and conservation planning** — all rooted in the incredible record preserved by museum collections.
